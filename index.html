More about my research and training

For the fellowship I screened 50 compounds in CMap against sex-biased genes from 44 GTEx tissues. Of 39,996 possible compound–lineage–tissue comparisons, 6,122 could be tested. Seventeen compounds showed enrichment in at least one tissue. When I counted the cell lines behind the strongest category, about 91% of its profiles came from female-donor lines, although it held 17 female-donor and 18 male-donor lines. The female-donor lines had been profiled about 11 times as often, so the screen could not separate sex from sampling. That sampling difference led me to ask what a donor label can tell us about the cells in culture.

Before that I was a research trainee at the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research’s Institute of Genomics and Integrative Biology (CSIR-IGIB) with Dr. Sherry Bhalla. I trained DeepTCR models using paired-chain sequencing data from 34 patients to study response to CD19 CAR-T therapy. When whole patients were held out, the model did not reliably predict response. The patient-held-out evaluation shows how the choice of split changed the result. I also helped update DeepTCR for Python 3.10 and TensorFlow 2.12, as noted in its release notes.

I earned my MSc in Computer Science: Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning from Woolf through Scaler Neovarsity, with a 4.0/4.0 GPA and 90 ECTS credits.

For the ADA Consortium’s Biomni-AD challenge I ran a Biomni agent workflow that proposed ten Alzheimer’s drug repurposing hypotheses. The entry was one of three winners of the Round 1 Biomni-AD Discovery Prize.

I also work on how language models behave. In TARA, I studied when a model attempts to call an irrelevant tool. In my BlueDot Impact sprint, I studied whether different kinds of refusal share a pattern in the model’s activations.

Computational biology

Sex-chromosome expression and annotated donor sex in drug-perturbation cell lines

Of 49 Tahoe-100M cell lines with a recorded donor sex, 27 lacked the expected Y-gene or XIST expression pattern. Bulk RNA sequencing from other cultures agreed with the measured patterns in 47 of 49 lines. The study compiled expression records for 1,123 cell lines.

Tests of gene-expression responses to 114 LINCS2020 compounds across 41 cell lines were inconclusive. The intervals were wide, and the XIST comparison also differed in the vehicle control. In the treated Tahoe cultures, COLO 205 accounted for 773 of 792 disagreements with the vehicle marker calls.

Methods and contributions

My part was the CMap and GTEx screen that came first (50 compounds, 44 tissues) and checking the GTEx sign convention. The Tahoe-100M and LINCS2020 analyses were planned, coded and run with Claude Code and rerun with OpenAI Codex, as the manuscript’s AI-use statement describes. Dr. Ben Guebila advised on the study design.

The Tahoe analysis scored eight Y-chromosome genes and XIST in vehicle-treated cells. The LINCS2020 analysis compared drug responses within 11 lineages using marker calls from bulk RNA sequencing. The two assays agreed on marker expression in 47 of 49 cell lines and on the state name in 46. A498 expressed XIST alone in both assays, but the two classification rules assigned it different names.

Predicting CD19 CAR-T response with DeepTCR

At CSIR-IGIB (Aug 2025 to Jan 2026, with Dr. Sherry Bhalla) I trained DeepTCR classifiers on beta-chain sequences from 34 patients with B-cell lymphoma, 18 of whom responded to CD19 CAR-T therapy. I also helped update the framework for Python 3.10 and TensorFlow 2.12.

When whole patients were held out, DeepTCR’s median AUROC was 0.518 across ten evaluations (range 0.386–0.737). The wide spread across splits of the same 34 patients leaves prediction in a new cohort unresolved.

How the validation changed the result

My trainee report gave an AUC of 0.754 when sequences from every patient appeared in both training and testing. The rerun, carried out with AI coding agents, grouped all 239,634 eligible records by patient. Across five partitions and two training seeds, DeepTCR outscored the matched sequence-composition baseline in nine of ten evaluations. On a separate five-fold split, it ranked 19 of 57 responder–nonresponder pairs correctly (AUROC 0.333).

For the ADA Consortium’s Biomni-AD challenge (March 2026) I ran a Biomni agent workflow, set up with Claude Code. AI interpretability

When does a language model call a tool it doesn’t need?

For TARA, I studied whether changing how tools are presented affects Qwen2.5’s decision to call one. On 32 requests where no tool applied, Qwen attempted 9 calls with a plain-text tool list and 16 with its native tool format. Claude Code drafted the original design and code.

In a follow-up designed and run by Codex, Qwen sometimes tried an out-of-area service when its documentation office was in the requested district. On 12 questions from independent benchmarks, moving the office to the requested city changed false attempts from four to three.

Activation steering and tool format

With the native tool format, steering Qwen’s activations at the strength selected during development (0.01) left false attempts at 2/8 and reduced correct calls on applicable requests from 7/8 to 6/8. Results

When does a model refuse a harmless question?

In my BlueDot Impact sprint, I compared four refusal directions in Qwen2.5. Intervening on each one also changed predictions on harmless text, leaving open whether any was specific to refusal.

Codex designed and ran a follow-up on 30 names. Qwen cited politics in 20 replies to “When did … become a billionaire?” and four to “In what year did … become a billionaire?” SmolLM2 gave no full refusals in 180 replies. Two blinded AI readers scored refusals. The dates were unverified.

Refusal measurement paper (PDF) Paired responses by person Code Results (JSON) Harmless-prompt check
Comparing the four refusal directions

The four directions had a mean cosine similarity of 0.84. Shuffling the category labels gave a mean of 0.98 across 300 permutations. Topic and wording could also contribute to the difference.
